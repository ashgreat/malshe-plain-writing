# Manuscript playbook

Read the part of the playbook for the section that you draft or revise. The
playbook lists, section by section, what the author does in his empirical
papers and the sentence patterns he uses for each step.

The patterns come from his published first-author papers in Journal of
Marketing (JM 2015) and Journal of Marketing Research (JMR 2020), from two
coauthored empirical papers (JM 2018 and an accepted manuscript for the
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, called the JAMS manuscript), and
from his current working manuscript. Quoted sentences carry a source tag.
Patterns with slots in square brackets are templates, and you fill the slots
with the content of the paper at hand. Templates marked "current" follow the
current manuscript.

Where his practice has changed over time, the playbook names the current
practice and the earlier one. Use the current practice unless the user's draft
or the target journal follows the earlier one. A rule that starts with
"Optional" may be dropped to meet a word limit.

## Budget

1. **Give each section its share of the body text.** In the two first-author
   papers the introduction takes 10% to 13%, the theory and the hypotheses 28%
   to 35%, the method 23% to 28%, the results 13% to 17%, and the discussion
   14% to 18%. The theory block is the longest section or ties with the
   method.

## Title

2. **Write a two-part title: a short phrase, a colon, and a literal
   subtitle.** In his first-author papers, the short phrase names the two
   domains or the two sets of actors that the paper connects. The subtitle
   lists the constructs in plain terms.
   Examples: "From Finance to Marketing: The Impact of Financial Leverage on
   Customer Satisfaction" (JM 2015). "How Main Street Drives Wall Street:
   Customer (Dis)satisfaction, Short Sellers, and Abnormal Returns" (JMR 2020).

3. **He also uses a fully literal title.** His coauthored papers have a
   literal main title and a subtitle that names the moderator or the type of
   contribution, e.g., "The Moderating Role of Hardware Lifecycle" and "A
   multi-level framework".

4. **Name the independent variable, the dependent variable, and the mediator
   or moderator in the subtitle.** A reader should know the model from the
   subtitle alone.

## Abstract

5. **Write one paragraph of about 150 to 180 words in the present tense.**

6. **Use the third person for JM and JMR.** The sentences start with "The
   authors examine", "They find", and "The authors empirically show". The
   journal style requires the third person in abstracts. Use "we" in the
   abstract when the target journal allows it.

7. **Follow sentence order A or sentence order B.** Use order B by default.
   Order A (JM 2015): what the authors examine; the headline finding; two or
   three sentences of mechanism; the main empirical result with the mediator;
   the moderators; the boundary result, introduced with "Finally,".
   Order B (JMR 2020): the gap in one "Although" sentence; what the authors
   examine; the data and the main finding in one sentence; the asymmetry or
   contrast; the moderators, introduced with "In addition,"; the magnitudes.

8. **State the gap in the first sentence when you use order B.**
   Example: "Although previous studies have established a direct link between
   customer-based metrics and stock returns, research is unclear on the
   mediated nature of their association." (JMR 2020)

9. **Put the data in a participial opener.**
   Example: "Using quarterly data from 273 firms over 2007–2017, the authors
   find that short interest—a measure of short seller activity—mediates the
   impact of customer satisfaction and dissatisfaction on abnormal stock
   returns." (JMR 2020)

10. **End the abstract with numbers when the paper has a clear magnitude.**
    Example: "The results show that a one-unit increase in customer
    satisfaction is associated with a .56 percentage point increase in abnormal
    returns, while a one-unit increase in customer dissatisfaction is
    associated with a 1.34 percentage point decrease in abnormal returns."
    (JMR 2020)

11. **Report only supported findings in the abstract.** The JM 2015 abstract
    does not mention the hypothesis that the data did not support.

12. **List four or five keywords as lowercase noun phrases.** Include the
    name of the research area when it applies, e.g., "marketing–finance
    interface".

## Introduction

The introduction runs seven paragraphs and takes a tenth to an eighth of the
body text, or 1,000 to 1,200 words. Its paragraphs have five to seven
sentences. It has no heading in a blind-review manuscript.

13. **Open with a general, citable statement about the standing of the
    topic.** The opening sentence is required. Do not open with an anecdote, a
    question, or a summary of one study. When the user gives no fact for the
    sentence, fill the current template with placeholders. The current
    manuscript also sets an epigraph from an institutional source above the
    first paragraph.
    Example: "Assessing the impact of customer satisfaction on various firm
    financial metrics is a central focus of marketing scholarship." (JMR 2020)
    Template: "The issues of [X] and [Y] have been central to [field] research
    for [period] (for a review, see [citation])."
    Template (current): "Understanding the relationship between [X] and [Y]
    continues to be a key concern for [named practitioner groups]
    ([citation])."

14. **Define the focal construct in the first paragraph, inside the sentence
    that introduces it.**
    Example: "The relative proportions of debt and equity constitute a firm's
    capital structure, typified by its financial leverage—the ratio of debt to
    firm value." (JM 2015)

15. **Summarize what prior research has established before you state the
    gap.** Use one or two sentences with citation strings. When the evidence is
    mixed, describe both sets of findings with parallel sentences.
    Example: "Multiple studies (Aksoy et al. 2008; Fornell et al. 2006;
    Fornell, Morgeson, and Hult 2016; Luo, Homburg, and Wieseke 2010; Raithel
    et al. 2012) report a significant and positive impact of customer
    satisfaction on abnormal returns." (JMR 2020)
    The next sentence starts with "In contrast, other studies" and ends with
    "reporting a null association."

16. **State the gap in one sentence.** The JM 2015 paper has a short sentence
    that starts with "Yet". When the evidence is mixed, the JMR 2020 paper
    closes the first paragraph with one sentence that says what the review
    shows and that more research is needed. The current manuscript describes
    the gap as mixed findings and a missing mechanism, with graded quantifiers
    such as "predominantly", "comparatively few", and "sparse". Do not write
    that no study exists unless the user confirms it.
    Examples: "Yet little research investigates the effects of debt on
    marketing." (JM 2015) "The conclusion that emerges from a careful review of
    studies is that the association between customer satisfaction and abnormal
    returns is not as well established as some may believe and that more
    research is needed to understand it." (JMR 2020)

17. **Say why the gap is surprising or costly, and count the reasons.** Follow
    the count with "First,", "Second,", and "Third,". Give each reason a fact
    with a citation, and a number when one exists. Rule 17 or rule 19 is
    required in every introduction. When the user gives no reasons, write the
    count sentence and the ordinals with placeholders.
    Example: "This is surprising for three reasons. First, debt, and not
    equity, is the preferred method of external financing for U.S. firms, with
    debt accounting for approximately 80% of all new external financing (Baker
    and Wurgler 2002)." (JM 2015)
    The example is from 2015. Write "This gap is surprising for three
    reasons." in new text.

18. **State the aim and the research questions.** End the lead-in sentence
    with a colon, set the questions off as a numbered list, and open the next
    paragraph with "To answer these questions, we". Write new questions with
    associational wording, e.g., "Is [X] associated with [Y]?" and "Does [M]
    moderate the association between [X] and [Y]?" Keep the questions inside
    the sentence with (1) and (2), as in the JM 2015 example, only when the
    user or the journal asks for it.
    Example: "we aim to answer two related questions: (1) Does leverage have a
    negative impact on customer satisfaction? and (2) Is the impact of
    customer satisfaction on firm value moderated by the firm's leverage?" (JM
    2015)
    The sentence opens with the purpose: "To further our understanding of the
    dynamics between leverage, customer satisfaction, and firm value,".

19. **Organize the middle of the introduction around the aspects of the
    study, as an alternative to rules 17 and 18.** Announce the count, and
    give each aspect its own paragraph. Each paragraph says what previous
    studies did, then what the present study does, then why the difference is
    useful.
    Example: "Four critical aspects of our study shed further light on this
    relationship. First, most previous studies explore only the direct link
    between customer satisfaction and abnormal returns." (JMR 2020)

20. **Name the theoretical lens in its own paragraph.** The JMR 2020 paper
    starts the paragraph with "Theoretically,". The JM 2015 paper has no lens
    paragraph in its introduction.
    Example: "Theoretically, we build on the motivation, ability, and
    opportunity (MAO) framework (Heider 1958; Wang, Gupta, and Grewal 2017) to
    argue that short sellers (1) are motivated to exploit any informational
    advantage in the stock market (Diamond and Verrecchia 1987), (2) possess
    superior ability in detecting investment opportunities (e.g., Engelberg,
    Reed, and Ringgenberg 2012), and (3) use the opportunity to gain an
    informational advantage relative to average investors (e.g., Denev and
    Amen 2020)." (JMR 2020)
    Template (current): "In this research, we posit that [thesis], a concept
    known in [theory] as [term] ([citation])."

21. **State the core argument in one sentence, and restate it once in
    different words when the argument is dense.**
    Example: "Our core argument is that an increase in customer satisfaction
    (dissatisfaction) lowers (elevates) short selling intensity in a firm's
    stock, which affects abnormal stock returns. Stated differently, this
    research examines the extent to which short selling mediates the
    association between customer satisfaction (and dissatisfaction) and
    abnormal returns." (JMR 2020)

22. **Preview the empirical work in a paragraph that starts with
    "Empirically," or "To answer these questions,".** Give the number of
    firms, the time span, and the number of observations. Name the model and
    the estimator, and say in one sentence how the paper handles
    identification. The JM 2015 preview gives only the firms, the years, and
    the model.
    Examples: "Empirically, we test these research questions using a sample of
    171 firms surveyed in the American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI) over
    17 years." (JM 2015) "Empirically, we use a multiple-source data set of
    273 firms spanning 2007 to 2017, yielding 7,383 firm-quarter
    observations." (JMR 2020)

23. **Preview the findings in the introduction.** Start with "We find that"
    or "Our results show that". State the direction of every finding, and give
    one magnitude for the headline effect in a form from the reporting file.
    Do not give a coefficient, a p-value, or a confidence interval in the
    introduction. Leave the magnitude out only when the user asks for
    directions only.
    Example: "The impact of leverage on satisfaction is economically
    significant: a one-standard-deviation increase in leverage from the average
    level results in a .47 point decrease in customer satisfaction, which is
    equivalent to an estimated loss of $26 million in net operating cash flows
    (Gruca and Rego 2005)." (JM 2015)
    The colon that joins the two clauses is the earlier practice. Write two
    sentences in new text.

24. **List the contributions with ordinals.** Announce the contributions in
    one sentence, then start each with "First,", "Second,", and "Finally,".
    Give each contribution two or three sentences. The first says what
    previous research did, with a citation. The second says what the paper
    shows, in the form "we contribute to the [name] literature by showing that
    [claim]". The last clause or sentence says what the result explains or
    which call for research it answers, with "which can explain" or "Thus,".
    When the user gives no prior work for a contribution, write [what previous
    research did] ([citation]).
    Examples: "We contribute to the marketing literature in several ways.
    First, although previous researchers have examined the impact of leverage
    changes resulting from equity offerings, we are among the first to study
    the impact of ongoing, more frequent, and more widely used debt sources on
    customer satisfaction and firm value." (JM 2015) "Finally, we contribute to
    the customer satisfaction literature by showing that leverage moderates the
    link between satisfaction and firm value, which can explain some of the
    wide variation in the relationship reported by Anderson, Fornell, and
    Mazvancheryl (2004)." (JM 2015)
    Template (current): "We contribute to [literature A] and [literature B].
    First, our findings show that [claim]. Second, we show how [claim]."

25. **Tie a contribution to a published call for research when one exists.**
    Example: "Thus, our research enhances understanding of the impact of
    financial decisions on marketing and answers the call for an increased
    understanding of marketing's interface with other business functions
    (Kumar 2015)." (JM 2015)

26. **Make priority claims only when the user confirms them.** The published
    papers say "we are among the first to study" and "our study is the first
    to replicate". The current manuscript makes no priority claim and uses
    "we show", "our findings show", and "we advance" as the claim verbs. Follow
    the current practice by default.

27. **Optional. Preview the managerial implications, and add a roadmap.** The
    JM 2015 paper has a paragraph that starts with "Our research has several
    managerial implications." and a roadmap paragraph that starts with "The
    rest of the article proceeds as follows." It returns to the implications
    in the discussion with more detail and the same opener. The JMR 2020 paper
    has neither paragraph, and its introduction ends with the empirical
    preview and the findings. The current manuscript also has neither. Leave
    both out by default.

## Conceptual background and literature review

28. **Organize the review by argument, not by author.** Each paragraph makes
    one claim and supports it with evidence.

29. **Open the theory section with a primer when the paper brings a construct
    from another field to marketing readers.** Say what the practice is, how
    it differs from its familiar opposite, how it is measured, and give one
    worked example. The JM 2015 paper does so for the benefits and costs of
    debt, and the JMR 2020 paper does so for short selling.

30. **Start each paragraph with a claim in plain words.**
    Examples: "Leverage can affect a firm's stakeholders in multiple ways."
    (JM 2015) "Higher leverage can also influence managers' incentives and
    actions." (JM 2015) "The finance literature has identified two major
    benefits of debt." (JM 2015)

31. **Alternate a claim that ends with a parenthetical citation and a
    sentence that has the cited authors as its subject.** With the authors as
    the subject, use "find", "show", "report", "argue", or "document".
    Examples: "Higher leverage can lower a firm's financial flexibility and
    pressure managers to meet debt obligations (Zingales 1998)." (JM 2015)
    "Matsa (2011) reports higher stockouts among heavily leveraged
    supermarkets." (JM 2015)

32. **Follow an abstract claim with a concrete case.** Use a named industry
    or a named firm, and start with "For example,".
    Example: "For example, buyers of automobiles need parts and services over
    a period of several years." (JM 2015) "Both General Motors and Chrysler
    lost a substantial number of customers when they went into bankruptcy in
    2009 (Frank 2009)." (JM 2015)

33. **Close an argument block with a consequence sentence.** Start it with
    "Thus,", "In summary,", "Therefore,", or "Overall,". About four theory
    paragraphs in ten end in that way in the JM 2015 paper, and two in ten in
    the JMR 2020 paper. Do not add the sentence to every paragraph.
    Example: "In summary, firms feel the pressure to maintain adequate cash
    flows to service debt, retain financial flexibility, and fund competitive
    strategies." (JM 2015)

34. **When the focal variable has an upside and a downside, give the upside
    first in a counted list, turn with one short sentence, and then count the
    downside.**
    Example: "However, debt has its costs as well." (JM 2015) The sentence
    follows the counted benefits of debt.

35. **Mark the step from other people's findings to the paper's own
    argument with a first-person sentence.**
    Examples: "We argue that marketers of leveraged firms will respond to the
    pressure on the firm to conserve cash." (JM 2015) "Correspondingly, we
    propose that the cash flow pressures faced by leveraged firms will also be
    transmitted to their marketing managers." (JM 2015)
    Templates (current): "We extend this logic to [context]." "Along the same
    lines, we argue that [claim]." "Building upon this argument, we propose
    [claim]."

36. **Define each construct in the first sentence of its subsection, with a
    citation.**
    Examples: "Competitive intensity is defined as the degree of competition
    faced by a focal firm (Grewal and Tansuhaj 2001)." (JMR 2020) "Capital
    intensity is the extent to which a firm makes investments in fixed assets
    (Erramilli and Rao 1993)." (JMR 2020)

37. **Explain an unfamiliar practice with a short worked example that uses
    round numbers.**
    Example: "As an example, for a firm with 100 million shares outstanding
    and 5 million of these shares shorted, the short interest is 5% (5
    million/100 million)." (JMR 2020)

38. **Raise the natural objection yourself and answer it.**
    Example: "Natural questions might arise regarding whether the financial
    leverage of a firm is important to a customer when making a buying decision
    and whether it can "directly" affect customer satisfaction." (JM 2015)
    The paragraphs that follow answer the question for business markets and
    then for consumer markets.

39. **Split a long argument into its links, or into the components of the
    named framework, and give each part a run-in heading.** End each part with
    an inference about the focal actor.
    Examples: "Leverage, advertising, and R&D." (JM 2015) "Motivation to
    gather and process information." (JMR 2020)

40. **Introduce the framework figure in one sentence.** The JM 2015 paper then
    walks through the links in the order of the model. The current manuscript
    writes one sentence with the figure as the subject and does not walk
    through the boxes.
    Example: "In this research, we focus on the influence of leverage on
    customer satisfaction and firm value through the relationships shown in
    Figure 1." (JM 2015)

41. **Close the section with a pointer to the next one.**
    Examples: "We next discuss these relationships in more detail." (JM 2015)
    "Next, we outline our argument for hypothesizing the moderating effects."
    (JMR 2020)

42. **Compare the paper with prior studies in a table when the literature is
    crowded.** The JMR 2020 paper has a table titled "Research in Marketing on
    the Association Between Customer Satisfaction and Financial Performance."
    with one row per study and a last row labeled "Current study". The current
    manuscript moves the comparison table to an appendix and points to it in
    one clause.

43. **State the limits of each construct where you introduce it.** Say in
    plain declarative sentences what falls outside the construct and what the
    measure cannot show. The current manuscript does so in the theory section
    and in the limitations.

## Hypothesis development

44. **Give each hypothesis group its own subsection, and name the relation in
    the heading.** The JM 2018 paper names the outcome and adds the labels,
    e.g., "Purchase Intent (Propositions 3 and 4)".
    Examples: "Moderators of the Leverage–Customer Satisfaction Link" (JM
    2015). "The Moderating Role of Competitive Intensity" (JMR 2020).
    "Mediating Role of Short Interest in Customer Satisfaction–Abnormal Returns
    Link" (JMR 2020).

45. **Build the argument as a chain with the construct as the subject.** In a
    moderator subsection, do not open with "We argue that", and do not state
    the prediction before the chain. The order is the definition, the first
    condition with cited evidence, its consequence, the second condition, the
    agreement with prior evidence, a summary sentence that starts with "In
    summary,", and the lead-in. The first person appears only in the lead-in.
    Give each condition its own paragraph when it has two sentences or more.
    Join the steps inside a condition with "because" in the middle of the
    sentence, and close the condition with "Thus,", "As such,", or
    "Consequently," at the start of a sentence.
    Examples: "When competitive intensity is high, rivals can more easily
    satisfy customers of a focal firm because their offerings are relatively
    more similar and interchangeable" (JMR 2020). "As such, changes in customer
    satisfaction should be less informative for short sellers' decisions and
    less likely to affect abnormal returns." (JMR 2020) "In summary, customer
    satisfaction has lower (higher) informational value for short sellers when
    firms operate in markets with high (low) competitive intensity." (JMR
    2020)
    Example of the agreement step: "These arguments are consistent with prior
    research showing that customer satisfaction has a weaker association with
    downstream metrics when competitive intensity is high (Anderson, Fornell,
    and Mazvancheryl 2004)." (JMR 2020)

46. **Hedge the mechanism and state cited findings without hedges.** Use
    "may", "can", "should", and "likely" for what the paper argues. Use the
    plain present tense for what cited studies found.
    Example: "In such markets, the marginal benefits to increasing customer
    satisfaction are likely to be lower." (JM 2015)

47. **Acknowledge the opposing argument and resolve it before the
    hypothesis.** Start the turn with "However," or "Yet". When research is
    divided, the JM 2018 paper argues both sides with "On the one hand" and
    "On the other hand" and then commits to one direction.
    Example: "However, Anderson, Fornell, and Rust (1997) propose that both
    customer satisfaction and productivity cannot be increased when customer
    satisfaction is relatively more dependent on customization." (JM 2015)
    Templates (current): "Prior research does show that [X], but [Y]."
    "Although there is evidence [X], it does not establish [Y]."

48. **End the argument with a short lead-in to the formal statement.**
    Examples: "Accordingly, we posit the following:" (JM 2015) "Along similar
    lines, we posit the following:" (JM 2015) "Yet given the trade-off between
    satisfaction and efficiency, we expect the following:" (JM 2015) "This
    leads us to our first proposition:" (JM 2018)
    The JMR 2020 paper ends the paragraph with the single word "Thus," and
    sets the hypothesis below it.
    Templates (current): "Based on this discussion, we expect that:" "We
    therefore expect:" "Therefore, we propose that:"

49. **Write the formal statement as one declarative sentence in the present
    tense.** Make it directional. Use the labels H1, H2a, and H2b, and split a
    hypothesis into parts (a), (b), and (c) when the parts share one argument.
    The frames in his papers are the comparison, the correlative frame "The
    higher [X], the higher [Y]", the ranking of several effects, and the
    moderation that is spelled out with "such that".
    Examples: "H2a: The negative effect of leverage on customer satisfaction
    is more negative for service firms than for manufacturing firms." (JM
    2015) "H1: The (negative) impact of higher leverage on customer
    satisfaction is mediated by a firm's (a) advertising expenditures and (b)
    R&D expenditures." (JM 2015) "P5: The higher the OSM, the higher the
    customer satisfaction." (JM 2018)
    The examples show the form of the statement. Their wording ("effect" and
    "impact") is the earlier practice. Use the wording of rule 50.

50. **Use associational wording for a hypothesis that observational data will
    test.** The JMR 2020 paper and the current manuscript write "is associated
    with". The JM 2015 paper and his coauthored papers write "impact" and
    "effect". Follow the later practice, and keep the earlier wording when the
    user's draft uses it throughout.
    Template: "H[n]: [X] is positively associated with [Y]."
    Template for a moderator: "H[n]: The association between [X] and [Y] is
    weaker for firms with higher [M]."

51. **Paired alternatives in parentheses can state both directions in one
    hypothesis.** The JMR 2020 paper and the JAMS manuscript do so in most
    hypotheses. The current manuscript does not use the device in a
    proposition. Use the device only when both directions are tested.
    Example: "H3: The impact of customer satisfaction on firm value is higher
    (lower) for firms with lower (higher) leverage." (JM 2015)

52. **Call the predictions hypotheses by default.** Use the labels H1 and H2a.
    Use propositions when the user's draft has them or the user asks for them.
    The JM 2018 paper and the current manuscript state propositions and test
    them, with the labels P1 and "Proposition 1".

53. **When theory supports both signs, write competing predictions.** The
    current manuscript says in the first sentence that the relation could go
    either way, argues the positive case, states the first prediction, then
    argues the negative case and states the second. When no relation is
    expected, it states the null as a numbered prediction and argues why the
    mechanism does not apply to that group.

54. **Keep parallel constructs in one fixed order everywhere.** List the
    actors, the outcomes, and the independent variables in the same order in
    the introduction, the theory, the hypotheses, the tables, and the results.

## Method

55. **Order the method section to fit the paper.** JM 2015 goes from model
    specification to data and variables to sample characteristics. JMR 2020
    goes from the empirical model to the treatment of selection and
    endogeneity, then estimation, data, measures, and controls. The current
    manuscript goes from data to measures to the model and the estimation
    strategy. Use the current order by default. A first-level heading for the
    theory or the method may be followed at once by its first second-level
    heading, with no overview paragraph.

56. **Start a sentence with its purpose when the reason for a step is not
    obvious.** Use "To [purpose], we [action]".
    Examples: "To test our hypotheses, we need data on customer satisfaction,
    advertising, R&D, firm value, leverage, and control variables." (JM 2015)
    "To address potential simultaneity, we followed Petersen and Kumar (2009)
    and selected an estimation model that jointly estimates all model
    coefficients." (JM 2015) "To formally test mediation, we test the
    statistical significance of the products of the coefficients of customer
    satisfaction and dissatisfaction in Equation 3, with the coefficient of
    short interest in Equation 2." (JMR 2020)

57. **Justify each method choice in the sentence that introduces it.** Use a
    precedent, a reason, or an equivalence.
    Precedent: "Following prior literature, we used Tobin's q as a measure of
    shareholder value (Anderson, Fornell, and Mazvancheryl 2004; Rao, Agarwal,
    and Dahlhoff 2004)." (JM 2015)
    Reason: "Because there is no common firm-level identifier between the ACSI
    and Compustat, we manually matched ACSI brands belonging to corresponding
    Compustat firms." (JM 2015)
    Template for an equivalence: "This approach is equivalent to [the familiar
    alternative] ([citation])."

58. **Name each data source in full at first use, with its abbreviation in
    parentheses.** Then use the abbreviation.
    Example: "The stock market data from the Center for Research in Security
    Prices (CRSP) are also available at a daily frequency." (JMR 2020)

59. **Describe the data assembly in order: sources, matching, filters, final
    sample.** Give the reason for each filter. State the final sample in one
    sentence with the number of observations, the number of firms, and the
    period.
    Examples: "We excluded financial firms (banking, insurance, and trading)
    from our analysis because they are subject to very different regulations
    than nonfinancial firms." (JM 2015) "The final, merged data set covers a
    period from Q2 2007 to Q4 2017, yielding 7,383 firm-quarter observations
    representing 273 firms." (JMR 2020)
    The word "very" in the first example is the earlier practice. Leave it out
    in new text.

60. **Give each measure its own run-in heading and define it in the first
    sentence.** State the formula in words, the direction of the coding, the
    sample mean, and the precedent.
    Examples: "We operationalized leverage as the ratio of a firm's total debt
    to the sum of total debt and market value of equity." (JM 2015) "Higher
    values of Competition indicate a more competitive industry." (JM 2015)
    "The average short interest for our sample is 5.2%, similar to the 4.9%
    average in Rapach, Ringgenberg, and Zhou (2016)." (JMR 2020)
    Template (current): "We measure [X] as [definition], which is the primary
    measure of [construct] in previous research (e.g., [citation])."

61. **Show a measure with one named firm.**
    Example: "For example, in the fourth quarter of 2017, Best Buy had 296
    million shares outstanding, out of which 27.635 million shares were
    shorted" (JMR 2020). The sentence then gives the resulting percentage.

62. **Explain why a measurement choice is better than the alternative.**
    Example: "Whereas the book value of equity is just an accounting
    adjustment (Welch 2004), the market value of equity reflects investors'
    belief about the worth of the firm" (JM 2015).

63. **Validate a new measure in the paper and say what the validation
    shows.** Describe the steps in order and close with the conclusion. The
    JMR 2020 paper compares a new satisfaction measure with an established one
    and concludes that the two measures capture the same construct.

64. **Introduce every equation with a sentence that ends in a colon, number
    it, and follow it with one "where" sentence that defines every symbol.**
    Examples: "Accordingly, we specify the following selection equation:" (JMR
    2020) "where, for each firm i and quarter t, SIit is short interest, CSit
    is customer satisfaction, CDit is customer dissatisfaction, COMPit is
    competitive intensity in the firm's industry, and CIit is the firm's
    capital intensity." (JMR 2020)
    Template: "We model [Y] as a function of [X], [M], and control variables:"

65. **Say what each coefficient must look like for each hypothesis to
    hold.** The JMR 2020 paper does so right after the equations.
    Template: "We expect positive and significant [coefficient 1] and negative
    and significant [coefficient 2] to support H[a] and H[b]."

66. **Present controls with their reasons.** Group the controls by equation.
    Give each control one sentence with a reason or a citation. The current
    manuscript puts all controls in one paragraph, and each sentence has the
    form "[Ordinal], we control for [X] because [reason] ([citation])."
    Example: "First, we controlled for institutional investors' ownership
    because they may have a shortsighted preference for firms with current
    earnings (Porter 1992), which can lead to lower advertising and R&D." (JM
    2015)

67. **Give identification its own heading.** Name each source of bias, e.g.,
    omitted variables, simultaneity, reverse causality, and selection. Give
    one remedy per source, with its citation.
    Example: "We took several steps to address endogeneity concerns resulting
    from potential omitted variables, simultaneity, and reverse causality."
    (JM 2015)

68. **Explain the logic of a correction in plain sentences before the
    technical detail.** Say what the fixed effects or the controls already
    handle, then what may remain, then how the paper handles the remainder.
    Examples: "Instruments must be theoretically linked to endogenous
    independent variables." (JMR 2020) "Thus, peers-of-peers firms affect focal
    firms only indirectly via their effect on the common peer firms." (JMR
    2020)

69. **Open the estimation subsection by counting and naming the equations.**
    List the features that the estimator must handle with "First,", "Second,",
    and "Third,", and then name the estimator with its citation.
    Example: "The empirical model has three equations: abnormal returns, short
    interest, and selection." (JMR 2020)

70. **Report each specification test and say in plain words what it
    means.**
    Example: "The nonsignificant test statistic" is followed by the values in
    parentheses and then "suggests that the SUR estimates are preferable to the
    estimates of instrumental regressions and that endogeneity is not a
    concern." (JM 2015)

71. **Describe the sample in a short paragraph.** Give the industry mix with
    percentages, the exclusions, and the final count.
    Example: "Utilities constitute a large proportion (20.58%) of the sample,
    followed by the retail (15.3%) and food (7.95%) industries." (JM 2015)

72. **Point to the descriptive table and report three to five facts from
    it.** Report correlations with r and p. Name a surprise when there is one.
    Examples: "Table 2 shows the summary statistics and correlations for the
    sample." (JM 2015) "Contrary to expectations, R&D intensity has no
    correlation with customer satisfaction" (JM 2015).

73. **Use the present tense for what the model and the measures are.** The JM
    2015 paper reports procedures in the past tense, e.g., "we used", "we
    obtained", and "we measured". The JMR 2020 paper and the current manuscript
    use the present tense, e.g., "we use", "we estimate", "we include", and
    "we control for". Follow the later practice. Keep the past tense for
    one-time actions on the data, e.g., "we manually matched" and "we gathered
    information".

74. **List the variables in a table.** The JMR 2020 table has the columns
    Variable, Purpose, Data Source, Definition, and Examples of Supporting
    Literature. Introduce the table in one sentence.
    Example: "Table 2 describes these variables, their source, and previous
    research using these variables." (JMR 2020)

## Results

75. **Open with the table and the overall fit or the model comparison.** A
    paper with time-series models may first show model-free evidence and the
    specification tests, each under a short heading, as the JM 2018 paper
    does. When the tables hold several estimators, say which estimates the
    text relies on and why.
    Examples: "In Table 3, we report the results of the SUR estimation." (JM
    2015) "Table 7 reports the comparison of the nested models." (JMR 2020)
    "Next, we turn to the results from our main model, as shown in Table 8."
    (JMR 2020)

76. **Organize the results by hypothesis group, in the order of the theory
    section.** The JM 2015 paper gives each group a subsection, reuses the
    wording of the theory heading, and adds the label. The JMR 2020 paper uses
    run-in headings such as "Test of hypotheses.".
    Example: theory heading "Moderators of the Leverage–Customer Satisfaction
    Link" and results heading "Moderation of the Customer Satisfaction–Firm
    Value Link (H3)" (JM 2015).

77. **Report each test by restating the hypothesis, showing the estimate, and
    stating the verdict.** Use that order when the paper has numbered
    hypotheses. When a paragraph or a subsection tests two or more hypotheses,
    state one combined verdict in its last sentence. A separate verdict
    sentence follows a single test only when the test has its own paragraph.
    Examples of restating: "H2b posits that the link between leverage and
    customer satisfaction will be more negative for firms operating in
    competitive industries." (JM 2015) "H4a and H4b hypothesize that short
    interest mediates the link between customer satisfaction/dissatisfaction
    and abnormal stock returns." (JMR 2020)
    Examples of the verdict: "In summary, we find empirical support for H2a
    and H2b but not for H2c." (JM 2015) "Thus, H1c is fully supported." (JMR
    2020) "Both H2a and H2b are supported." (JMR 2020)

78. **He also puts the verdict first.** His coauthored papers join the verdict
    to the restatement and then give the estimate. The same papers report
    partial support in that form.
    Templates: "We find support for [P1] in that [finding] ([statistics])."
    "In support of [H1], we find that [estimate] ([p-value])." "Therefore, we
    find partial support for [P6] in that [finding]."
    Template (current), for propositions grouped by independent variable:
    "[X] is positively associated with [Y1] (β = ..., p < ...) and [Y2] (β =
    ..., p < ...), consistent with Propositions [a] and [b]."

79. **Vary the verb that restates a hypothesis.** He uses "posits",
    "proposes", "hypothesizes", "pertain to", and "according to".

80. **Add one clause of interpretation after the estimate.** The clause says
    what the estimate means for the actors or the mechanism, and it is hedged
    with "may" or "suggests". Do not restate the hypothesis in the clause.
    Start it with "which indicates that", "indicating that", or "suggesting
    that".
    Example: "which indicates that the leverage-induced cash flow pressure may
    force service firms to pursue productivity gains at the cost of lower
    customer satisfaction" (JM 2015).

81. **Report an unsupported hypothesis plainly.** Say what the data show,
    state the verdict in one sentence, and point to where the paper discusses
    it. Do not write that a hypothesis is rejected.
    Example: "We elaborate on this counterintuitive result in the "Discussion"
    section and provide guidance for further research." (JM 2015)
    Templates: "Thus, we do not find support for H[n]." "Our results do not
    provide consistent evidence for H[n]."

82. **Name an unexpected result as unexpected.**
    Example: "Cash flow negatively influences advertising intensity" is
    followed by the statistics and by "which is an unexpected result." (JM
    2015)

83. **Keep the results section in the units of the model.** Neither
    first-author paper translates a coefficient into dollars or points inside
    the results section. The translation of the headline effect goes in the
    abstract, the introduction, and the discussion.

84. **Probe an interaction with a named technique and report the thresholds
    in plain numbers.** He uses floodlight analysis, Johnson-Neyman plots, and
    spotlight analysis at two standard deviations from the mean.
    Example: "When leverage is at approximately 65%, customer satisfaction has
    no impact on Tobin's q." (JM 2015)

85. **Report mediation with the method sentence first and the intervals
    second.**
    Example: "Following Preacher and Hayes (2004), we draw 1,000 bootstrap
    samples with replacement to obtain the 95% confidence intervals for the
    indirect effect." (JMR 2020)

86. **Discuss control variables briefly or not at all.** The JM 2015 paper
    gives one short paragraph to the notable controls. The current manuscript
    reports none in the text.

87. **Write robustness checks as a counted list or as run-in paragraphs.**
    A short set goes in one paragraph that starts with the count. A long set
    goes in separate paragraphs, and each paragraph has a run-in heading that
    names the check. Each check says what changed and why, then the verdict,
    then the table location. A check may open with what the main analysis did
    and why, and then turn with "Nonetheless,".
    Examples: "We performed three robustness checks." (JM 2015) "As Web
    Appendix Table W10 shows, our results continue to hold." (JMR 2020)
    "Nonetheless, we include these variables as controls and find that the
    results continue to hold (see Web Appendix Table W8)." (JMR 2020)

88. **State the verdict of a robustness check in one of his forms.** "We find
    that our results continue to hold." "We find that our results are largely
    unchanged." "Our results are not sensitive to this alternative". "The main
    results are unchanged." "The results remain unchanged." State an exception
    in the next sentence, and start it with "Only".

## Discussion

89. **Start the discussion with a summary, with the primary contribution, or
    with the first theoretical contribution.**
    Summary opening (JM 2015): restate the aim in one sentence and the
    findings in four or five sentences, then point to the implications.
    Example: "This research examines the impact of leverage on customer
    satisfaction and firm value. Our results indicate that leverage affects
    customer satisfaction negatively and is mediated by advertising
    expenditures." (JM 2015)
    Contribution opening (JMR 2020): "This article's primary contribution is
    to disentangle the apparent lack of value relevance of customer
    satisfaction for abnormal returns." (JMR 2020)
    Direct opening (current): go straight to the first theoretical
    contribution with "We advance [theory] by demonstrating that [claim]."

90. **Divide the discussion into theoretical implications, managerial
    implications, and limitations with future research.** The headings in the
    published papers are "Theoretical Implications", "Managerial
    Implications", "Limitations and Further Research", "Implications for
    Business Executives and Regulators", and "Limitations". The papers have no
    separate conclusion section.

91. **Write each theoretical implication as a claim, what previous research
    did, and what the paper shows.** A closing sentence that starts with
    "Thus," and names the contribution is optional. The paragraph may end with
    one sentence on what future research should do on that point.
    Examples: "Thus, we contribute to this literature by identifying a
    different mechanism for the effect of a firm's capital structure on its
    marketing strategy." (JM 2015) "Future research should examine additional
    customer metrics that can provide an information advantage to short
    sellers and thus more deeply explicate the marketing–finance interface."
    (JMR 2020)

92. **Restate the headline magnitude in the discussion, in the sentence frame
    of the abstract and the introduction.**
    Example: "The value reduction due to customer dissatisfaction is 2.39
    times larger than the value enhancement due to an increase in customer
    satisfaction." (JMR 2020)

93. **Address managerial implications to named roles.** He names CMOs, CFOs,
    executives, marketers, regulators, shareholders, and investors. Announce
    the implications, and mark each with an ordinal.
    Example: "Our research has several concrete implications for marketing
    managers. The first implication is valuable to CMOs in their dialogue with
    CFOs." (JM 2015)

94. **Build each implication as a finding, an inference verb, and an
    action.** Use "suggests that", "implies that", or "shows that", and then
    "should", "must", "can", or "need to".
    Example: "Third, our finding that leverage affects customer satisfaction
    more negatively for firms in competitive industries implies that marketers
    in such industries must keep a close eye on their leveraged competitors."
    (JM 2015)
    The phrase "keep a close eye on" is a figure of speech. Write "monitor" in
    new text.

95. **Make the action concrete.** Name the industry, the metric, or the
    decision.
    Example: "As an example, executives in industries with low competitive
    intensity, such as prepackaged software and system design (Standard
    Industrial Classification codes 7372/7373), will find their stock returns
    to be more sensitive to changes in customer satisfaction than executives in
    industries such as financial services (an industry with higher competitive
    intensity)." (JMR 2020)

96. **Say what goes wrong when managers ignore the finding.**
    Example: "First, executives who ignore short selling may underestimate the
    magnitude of the impact of customer satisfaction on abnormal stock returns
    and thus underinvest in customer satisfaction initiatives." (JMR 2020)

97. **Optional. Add an implications table.** The current manuscript has a
    table with the columns Finding, Implication for Executives, and Relevant
    Executives. Each implication in the table starts with a verb such as
    "Monitor", "Report", or "Track".

98. **Pair each limitation with a direction for research.** Number the
    limitations in a short paragraph, or give each its own short paragraph.
    Examples: "Clearly, our findings are limited by the sample of the ACSI
    firms available to us that are also publicly listed on the U.S. stock
    markets." (JM 2015) "Further research could explore industry-level dynamics
    by using data sources other than ACSI." (JM 2015) "Third, our empirical
    analysis is conducted using quarterly data due to the nature of the data
    available to us." (JMR 2020)
    Leave out "Clearly," in new text.
    Template (current): "Our research also has a few limitations which suggest
    potential research extensions. First, [limitation]. Future research could
    [remedy]."

99. **Offer a candid explanation for a puzzling result, and say what stops
    the paper from testing it.**
    Example: "Data limitations do not allow us to explore this possibility,
    but we hope that further research can resolve this issue." (JM 2015)

100. **Write about future research with the forms below.** "Future research
     should examine". "Future research could explore". "Future studies should
     examine". "Research using higher frequency data may provide". The JM 2015
     paper writes "Further research", and the later papers write "Future
     research".

## Headings, tables, appendix, and footnotes

101. **Write every heading as a noun phrase.** Do not write a heading as a
     question or a full sentence. Do not number the headings unless the
     journal does.

102. **Use title case for the first two heading levels in a manuscript for JM
     or JMR.** The first level names the section, e.g., "Conceptual
     Framework", "Methodology and Data", "Results", and "Discussion". The
     second level names the constructs and the relation. Follow the target
     journal when it prints sentence-case headings.

103. **Write third-level headings as run-in headings.** Use italics and
     sentence case, end the heading with a period, and continue with the text
     on the same line.
     Examples: "Measuring financial leverage." (JM 2015) "Sales growth." (JM
     2015) "Alternative estimation method." (JMR 2020)

104. **Make the exhibit the subject of a present-tense sentence.**
     Examples: "Table 1 provides a description of these variables and the
     source of the specific data items." (JM 2015) "Figure 3 visually
     summarizes the results of the mediation analysis" (JMR 2020).
     He also writes "As Tables 4 and 5 show," and "(see Table W17)".

105. **Move supporting detail to the Web Appendix and point to it in one
     sentence.**
     Examples: "More details of these imputations are available in the Web
     Appendix." (JM 2015) "A plot of these estimates, along with the 90%
     confidence interval band, is available in the Web Appendix." (JM 2015)

106. **Keep footnotes few and short.** The first-author papers have four or
     five each, and the current manuscript has nine. He uses a footnote to
     define a term, to add a side argument, to justify a functional form, to
     report a side test, or to thank a reviewer for a suggestion.
     Example: "Free cash flow is defined as the cash in excess of that
     required to fund all positive new present value projects." (JM 2015)

107. **Put estimation caveats in the table notes.** The notes carry the
     significance legend, what the parentheses hold, the estimator, and any
     scaling of the coefficients.
     Example: "Notes: t-statistics appear in parentheses." (JM 2015)
