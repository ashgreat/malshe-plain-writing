# Reporting formats and phrase bank

Read this file when you write results, methods, citations, or any sentence
that reports a number in an academic manuscript. It gives the exact formats
the author uses and the phrases that recur in his papers.

Quoted sentences carry a source tag: JM 2015 or JMR 2020. Patterns with slots
in square brackets are templates. Several formats come from journal house
style, and the file says so. When the target journal has a different house
style, the journal wins.

## Statistics in running text

1. **Put the estimate and its p-value in parentheses right after the claim
   they support.** Add the table and column when the reader may need to find
   the number.
   Examples: "We find that customer satisfaction has a positive impact on
   Tobin's q (b = .021, p ≤ .01; Table 3, Column 4)." (JM 2015) "The natural
   log of sales in Equation 4 is statistically significant (.348, p < .01),
   indicating that firms with higher sales have a higher probability of YouGov
   coverage." (JMR 2020)
   Template (current): "[X] is positively associated with [Y] (β = [value],
   p < [threshold])."

2. **Report p-values as thresholds.** Use p < .01, p < .05, and p < .10, and
   p > .10 for a null result. Give an exact p-value for a test of a difference
   between two coefficients, e.g., "p = .028".

3. **Drop the leading zero for a statistic that cannot exceed 1.** Write
   ".47", "p < .05", and "r = –.31". The rule comes from JM and JMR house
   style. Follow the target journal.

4. **Use these words for significance.** "significant", "marginally
   significant" for a result between .05 and .10, and "statistically
   nonsignificant" or "not significant" for a null result. He writes
   "nonsignificant" and does not write "insignificant".

5. **State the sign and the significance together.**
   Examples: "The interaction of customer satisfaction and competition is
   significant and positive" (JMR 2020). "must be negative and significant"
   (JM 2015).

6. **Give test statistics with their degrees of freedom.**
   Templates: "(χ2([df]) = [value], p > .10)", "(t = [value], p ≤ .05)", and
   "(r = [value], p ≤ .01)".

7. **Report a confidence interval with its level and its method, and say
   whether it includes zero.**
   Template: "([estimate], 95% bootstrap CI = [[lower], [upper]])".
   Example of the method sentence: "Following Preacher and Hayes (2004), we
   draw 1,000 bootstrap samples with replacement to obtain the 95% confidence
   intervals for the indirect effect." (JMR 2020)

8. **Report model fit in one sentence and say what it suggests.**
   Example: "The R2 values range from .30 to .89, suggesting excellent model
   fit." (JM 2015)

9. **Report a product of coefficients by showing the product.**
   Template: "(b = [product] = [a] × [b])".

10. **Translate the headline effect into a magnitude.** Use one of these
    templates.
    "a one-standard-deviation increase in [X] from the average level results
    in a [n] point decrease in [Y], which is equivalent to an estimated loss of
    $[n] million in [metric] ([citation])".
    "a one-unit increase in [X] is associated with a [n] percentage point
    increase in [Y]".
    "[effect A] is [n] times larger than [effect B]".
    Example: "Specifically, a one-unit increase in customer satisfaction is
    associated with a .56 percentage point increase in abnormal returns, while
    a one-unit increase in customer dissatisfaction is associated with a 1.34
    percentage point decrease in abnormal returns." (JMR 2020)

11. **Say plainly what a specification test means.**
    Templates: "suggesting that the instruments are valid", "which further
    confirms that multicollinearity is not an issue in this analysis", and
    "indicating that [plain statement]".

## Numbers

12. **Spell out one through nine when you count things, and use numerals from
    10 up.** He writes "five equations", "three robustness checks", and "two
    related questions", and he writes "171 firms", "17 years", and "23
    industries".

13. **Always use numerals with units, percentages, money, sample sizes, and
    statistics.** He writes "5%", "$26 million", "100 million shares", and
    "2 SDs".

14. **Use the percent sign, not the word.** He writes "80%" and "(20.58%)".

15. **Use a comma in numbers of four digits or more.** He writes "1,346
    firm-year observations" and "7,383 firm-quarter observations".

16. **Hyphenate a number phrase that modifies a noun.** He writes
    "one-standard-deviation increase", "one-unit increase", "17-year period",
    "one-year sales growth", "four-factor model", and "firm-quarter
    observations".

17. **Mark approximations with "approximately".** He writes "approximately
    80%" and "approximately 200 brands". He does not write "around" or "about"
    for a statistic, and he does not use a tilde.

18. **Give a period as a span of years.** In typeset articles the span has an
    en dash, e.g., "1994–2010". In running text he also writes "spanning 2007
    to 2017" and "from Q2 2007 to Q4 2017".

19. **Do not start a sentence with a numeral.** Reword the sentence or start
    with the unit of analysis.

## Citations

The citation format follows the target journal. The points below describe JM
and JMR style, which most of his papers use.

20. **Use author and year with no comma between them.** "(Kurt and Hulland
    2013)".

21. **Order multiple citations alphabetically and separate them with
    semicolons.** "(Gupta 2009; Myers 1977; Srivastava, Shervani, and Fahey
    1998)".

22. **Name all authors for up to three, and use "et al." from four.**
    "Anderson, Fornell, and Mazvancheryl (2004)" and "Fornell et al. 2006".

23. **Put "e.g.," inside the parentheses when the citation is one example
    among many.** "(e.g., Kurt and Hulland 2013)". He does not write "e.g."
    outside parentheses. Outside parentheses he writes "for example" or "such
    as".

24. **Point to a review with "for a review, see".** "(for a review, see
    Graham and Leary 2011)". He also writes "(see, e.g., [citation])" and
    "(cf. [citation])".

25. **Give the page for a direct quotation.** "Anderson and Sullivan (1993,
    p. 125) note that".

26. **Use a possessive for a named model or method.** "Carhart's (1997)
    four-factor model" and "Sobel's test".

27. **Support nearly every claim about prior work with a citation.** A claim
    about a stream of work gets a string of two to five citations.

28. **For a journal that uses APA style, switch the format and keep the
    habits.** His JAMS and Elsevier papers use commas and ampersands, e.g.,
    "(Angrist & Pischke, 2008)". Rules 23 to 27 still apply.

## Cross-references

29. **Capitalize the names of numbered items.** Table 3, Figure 1, Equation
    4, Column 2, Model 1, Panel A, Web Appendix, H1a, and Proposition 2.

30. **Use these forms to cite an exhibit.**
    "In Table 3, we report [content]."
    "Table 2 shows [content]."
    "[claim] (Table 3, Column 4)."
    "as shown in Table 8"
    "As Tables 4 and 5 show, [claim]."
    "(see Web Appendix Table W8)"

31. **Refer to equations by number and to sections by name.** "in Equation 2
    (Equation 3)" and "in the "Discussion" section".

## Abbreviations and terms

32. **Define an abbreviation at first use, in parentheses after the full
    term.** "research and development (R&D)", "American Customer Satisfaction
    Index (ACSI)", "chief marketing officers (CMOs)", and "seemingly unrelated
    regression (SUR)". Use the abbreviation from then on.

33. **Spell out the term when it starts a sentence.** He writes "Research and
    development has been shown to" and "Chief financial officers frequently
    use these methods" at the start of sentences, after the abbreviations are
    defined. The habit comes from JM house style.

34. **Introduce a method or a package with its citation.** "the conditional
    mixed process (CMP; Roodman 2011)".

35. **Put quotation marks around a term on first use when the term is used in
    a special sense.** He does so for "discipline", "option value", "real
    options", "underinvestment problem", "slack", and "information
    intermediary". Do not repeat the quotation marks on later uses.

36. **Keep one word per concept.** He writes "firm" throughout and rarely
    "company". He writes "customer satisfaction" in full on almost every use.
    Choose the term once and keep it. See the word rules in SKILL.md.

37. **Name variables in equations with short capital labels and define them
    in the "where" sentence.** CS, ADV, LEV, SI, and COMP. When a variable name
    appears in running text, capitalize it, e.g., "Higher values of
    Competition".

## Lists inside sentences

38. **Number the items of an inline list with (1), (2), and (3).** Use the
    form for conditions, research questions, and the parts of an argument.
    Example: "In this section, we propose three potential moderators that can
    influence the leverage–customer satisfaction link: (1) service versus
    manufacturing firms, (2) competitive intensity in the industry, and (3)
    sales growth." (JM 2015)

39. **Use (a) and (b) for the parts of one hypothesis.**

40. **Use the serial comma.**

## Punctuation in manuscripts

41. **Em dash.** He uses a closed em dash, with no spaces, to set off a short
    definition or a short list inside a sentence. The rate is about one per
    1,000 words or lower. He does not use an em dash for a pause or for
    emphasis.
    Example: "Second, the dependent variable in the selection equation is
    dichotomous, while the other two dependent variables—short interest and
    abnormal returns—are continuous." (JMR 2020)

42. **En dash.** He uses an en dash for a relation between two constructs,
    for a range, and for a method named after two people. Examples are "the
    customer satisfaction–firm value link", "the marketing–finance interface",
    "1994–2010", "H2a–c", "Equations 1–3", and "the Durbin–Wu–Hausman test".

43. **Colon.** He uses a colon before a numbered list, before numbered
    research questions, before a formal hypothesis, and before an equation.
    The JM 2015 paper also uses a colon four times to join a claim to its
    elaboration. The later papers do not. Do not join two clauses with a
    colon.

44. **Semicolon.** He uses semicolons between citations, between the items
    of a list that have commas inside them, and now and then between two
    parallel clauses.
    Example of parallel clauses: "H3a proposes that the association of
    unexpected changes in customer satisfaction and short interest is more
    (less) negative for firms with lower (higher) capital intensity; H3b
    posits that the association of unexpected changes in customer
    dissatisfaction and short interest is more (less) positive for firms with
    lower (higher) capital intensity." (JMR 2020)

45. **Parentheses.** He uses parentheses for abbreviations, for short lists
    of examples that start with "e.g.,", for statistics, for a short gloss,
    and for mirrored alternatives such as "higher (lower)".

46. **Slash.** He uses a slash rarely, for a ratio or for a fixed pair, e.g.,
    "customer satisfaction/dissatisfaction".

47. **He does not use exclamation marks, ellipses for effect, bold for
    emphasis, or bullet lists in the body of a manuscript.**

## Phrase bank

The phrases below recur in his papers. Use them where they fit. Do not use
more than one phrase from the same group in one paragraph.

### Stating what prior work has done

- "A large body of research in marketing has demonstrated that"
- "Previous research shows that" and "Previous research has shown that"
- "Extant research has demonstrated that"
- "Prior literature has suggested"
- "A stream of finance literature has argued that"
- "Research in finance has confirmed that"
- "Marketing scholars have argued that"
- "it is well documented in finance literature that"
- "Research shows that"
- "In a survey of [n] [respondents], [Authors] ([year]) find that"
- "Several studies have also examined"

### Stating the gap and the position of the paper

- "Yet little research investigates"
- "research is unclear on"
- "is an open issue and has not been investigated"
- "most previous studies explore only"
- "prior studies have assumed"
- "To the best of our knowledge,"
- "We build on their study by arguing that"
- "In contrast to these studies, the current study uses"
- "this research fits with recent studies that investigate"

### Moving to the paper's own argument

- "We argue that"
- "We posit that" and "we posit the following:"
- "we propose that"
- "we expect" and "we expect the following:"
- "we do not anticipate"
- "Our core argument is that"
- "Our logic is that"
- "Along similar lines,"
- "In a similar vein,"
- "Correspondingly,"
- "Consistent with this logic,"
- "Following this discussion,"

### Agreement and disagreement with prior work

- "consistent with"
- "in line with"
- "In support of this notion,"
- "Our result substantiates previous research showing that"
- "Our results lend support to the findings of"
- "Our results are consistent with [Author] ([year]), who argues that"
- "Echoing their work,"
- "Contrary to expectations,"
- "in contrast to"

### Cause and consequence inside a sentence

- "which in turn"
- "thereby"
- "leading to"
- "resulting in"
- "due to"
- "such that"
- "in that"
- "because" in the middle of a sentence, and "Because" at the start. He does
  not use "since" for cause.

### Starting a sentence that concludes

- "Thus,"
- "Therefore,"
- "Accordingly,"
- "Consequently,"
- "As such,"
- "In summary,"
- "Overall,"
- "Taken together,"

### Starting a sentence that adds, contrasts, or narrows

- Adds: "In addition,", "Furthermore,", "Moreover,", "Similarly,", "Also"
  inside the sentence, and "Finally," for the last item.
- Contrasts: "However,", "In contrast,", "Yet", "Nonetheless,", "Whereas",
  "Although", and "While".
- Narrows: "Specifically,", "For example,", "As an example,", "Indeed,", and
  "More generally," for the opposite move.
- Orders: "First,", "Second,", "Third,", "Fourth,", "Finally,", and "Next,".

### Reporting findings

- "We find that"
- "We also find that"
- "Our results show that", "Our results indicate that", and "Our result
  suggests that"
- "In support of H1a and H1b,"
- "Consistent with this expectation, we find that"
- "However, in contrast to H2c, we find that"
- "contrary to our conjecture"
- "The results support H1a."
- "more pronounced for"
- "economically significant"
- "We find that our results continue to hold."

### Limits and future work

- "Clearly, our findings are limited by"
- "Data limitations do not allow us to"
- "It is possible that"
- "A potential explanation for this puzzling result is that"
- "Although this is not the focus of our research,"
- "Further research could explore", "Further research should examine", and
  "Future studies should aim to"

### Words and phrases he does not use

- He does not use these words in any of his first-author papers or in the
  current manuscript: "delve", "tapestry", "landscape", "pivotal", "realm",
  "foster", "underscore", "showcase", "seamless", "intricate", "testament",
  "harness", "unlock", and "navigate".
- He does not write "insignificant" for a statistical result, "since" for
  cause, "Hence", "In sum,", or "etc." in running prose. He writes "and so on"
  rarely.
- He does not use contractions, the first person singular, or exclamation
  marks in a manuscript.

## Changes in his wording over time

- **From "impact" to "association".** The JM 2015 paper writes "impact",
  "effect", and "link" for the main relations. The JMR 2020 paper and the
  current manuscript write "association" and "is associated with" for
  estimates from observational data. Follow the later practice.
- **From past to present tense in methods.** See rule 67 in the manuscript
  playbook.
- **Fewer intensifiers.** "very", "highly", "extremely", and "Clearly," appear
  in the JM 2015 paper and almost never in the current manuscript. Leave them
  out.
- **Fewer bare demonstratives.** The published papers sometimes start a
  sentence with "This is" or "This suggests". The current manuscript attaches
  a noun every time, e.g., "This finding suggests". Attach the noun.
- **"dataset" as one word.** The JM and JMR papers print "data set" because of
  house style. The current manuscript writes "dataset". Follow the target
  journal.
